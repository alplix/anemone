Unit 1: Foundations: Society, Communication and Models

The Chicago School and Communication

For the Chicago School, society exists by making what is common through communication: the city is the laboratory of this process, and news and the immigrant press are its instruments; how people define a situation (the Thomas theorem) shapes behaviour, but the school’s concepts should be read in their historical context and with their limits.

Context and origins

Story

Cigarette paper back home, a subscription in America

June 1919. Mark Villchur, editor of the Russian-language paper Russkoye Slovo in New York, publishes the results of a survey of his readers. The question is simple: did you read newspapers in the old country? Of 312 replies, only 16 say “regularly”. Ten used to leaf through a paper now and then at the village administrative centre, and twelve subscribed to a weekly magazine. In America, all of them subscribe to or read Russian papers.

Some replies are more colourful still: people from Grodno, Minsk and Volhynia write that at home they used newspapers as cigarette paper, and that here they have become regular readers. Same paper, different job. A quarter of the readers also glance at American papers in English; some say they understand “only one word out of five”, others read only the headlines, because headlines are easy to follow.

The sociologist Robert Park used this survey both in his 1922 book and in his 1923 article. Our reading: because it shows something surprising; people who arrive in a new country do not stop reading, they start. Park’s explanation is short: news is urgent information used by a person trying to adjust to a new environment, and America offered a great deal to learn and habits to change. This lesson is the story of that idea and of the school that produced it.

Story

From one million to three: sociology steps out onto the street

In the 1890 census Chicago was the second-largest city in the United States: 1,099,850 people. In 1930: 3,376,438. More than a threefold rise in forty years. From 1890 to 1920, about three-quarters of Chicagoans were either immigrants or the children of immigrants; in 1900 the share approached 80 per cent. The immigrant population peaked in 1930: about 859,000 foreign-born plus 1.3 million children of immigrants, some 65 per cent of the city. The city’s newspapers matched: a later survey collected translations from the papers of Czech, Polish, Lithuanian, Norwegian, Russian, Swedish and many other communities.

In this city, in 1892, Albion Small founded the first sociology department in the United States; in 1895 he launched the American Journal of Sociology. In the period from about 1915 to 1935, social scientists at Chicago pioneered empirical research and a variety of qualitative and quantitative methods; today we call this the “Chicago School”.

Our interpretation: a city growing this fast, speaking this many languages, carried the question “what holds society together?” out of the pages of books and onto the street. The question of this lesson is: why did Chicago’s sociologists keep arriving at “communication” as the answer?

Story

“Send us your letters”: 10 to 20 cents each

  1. A strange advertisement appears in one of the Polish-language dailies, Dziennik Związkowy: anyone who has letters from the old country should send them to the advertiser; the aim is to show the nature of the Polish people. Ten to twenty cents will be paid per letter, and the letters will be returned. (Dziennik Związkowy was the daily paper of the Polish National Alliance.)

One reader, an immigrant with more than a hundred letters, writes a wary reply: is the reward real, will he get the letters back? The correspondence is valuable to him; when the war is over he expects money to collect or a farm to take over. “Send me a guarantee,” he says. In a letter two weeks later he writes that he trusts their generosity, and sends the letters.

The advertisers were W. I. Thomas and Florian Znaniecki, and the result was The Polish Peasant in Europe and America. The book contains series of letters; in one, someone living in Poland advises subscribing to a Polish weekly, Gazeta Świąteczna, saying that papers now print more truth because they appear without censorship. So the newspaper is inside the data, too.

For sociology this was a threshold: not only censuses and theories but documents that ordinary people wrote in their own words counted as data. A Turkish encyclopaedia entry says the study laid the foundation of qualitative research methods in American sociology.

Deep dive

Deep dive: the route of Park the reporter

Robert Ezra Park was born on 14 February 1864 in Harveyville, Pennsylvania; he grew up in Red Wing, Minnesota, where his family had moved. He studied philosophy at the University of Michigan, and one of his teachers was John Dewey.

From 1887 to 1898 he worked as a journalist: police stories, general assignments and features for papers in Minneapolis, Detroit, Denver, New York and Chicago. One source says he became known for the persistence with which he pursued a story and for writing about the underworld. Then he turned to academia: he finished a master’s at Harvard in 1899, attended Georg Simmel’s lectures in Berlin, and in 1904 took his doctorate at Heidelberg with a thesis titled Masse und Publikum (Crowd and Public). Our interpretation: the 1921 textbook has a section called “the crowd and the public” in its chapter on collective behaviour, which suggests that for the former reporter “public” and “news” were never foreign ideas.

Then he worked in public relations at the Tuskegee Institute for Booker T. Washington. In 1914 he joined the sociology department at the University of Chicago, and developed a “human ecology” approach that used the city as a research laboratory. After leaving his Chicago post he taught at Fisk University in Nashville and died there in 1944.

Note: the sources disagree on the years in which Park’s Tuskegee and Chicago posts ended, so we have not given those two years.

The core claim

Card

The core claim: society exists in communication

If you had to squeeze the Chicago School’s thinking on communication into one sentence: society is possible because people make something common, and the way to make it common is communication. So communication is not a tool added to society afterwards; it is society itself.

Park described this as a balance of two forces. Competition separates people: it brings an orderly distribution and a division of labour, and increases individuation. Communication builds understanding and cultural solidarity; it integrates. The two complement each other. Our interpretation: the city is one of the places where these two forces are most visible, and the city’s newspaper is communication’s most visible instrument. In 1915 Park calls the newspaper the great medium of communication within the city and writes that public opinion rests on the information it supplies.

Keep three ideas in mind:

  1. Society = a common world. Without a common language, common news and common attention there is no “we”.
  2. City = laboratory. Social processes appear enlarged there, so that is where they are studied.
  3. News = a form of knowledge. A kind of knowledge with its own rules, between acquaintance and formal knowledge.

We will see in turn how this ambitious sentence works, where it struggles, and who criticised it.

Card

The city as laboratory: why the city?

The last sentences of Park’s 1915 article “The City” are the nearest thing to a Chicago School slogan. A great city lays bare, in a massive way, traits that usually stay hidden and suppressed in small communities: it shows the good and the evil in human nature “in excess”. For Park this justifies the view that treats the city as a “laboratory or clinic” in which human nature and social processes can be studied most conveniently and profitably.

The laboratory here is not a tightly controlled experimental room. Our reading: the city makes everything visible, and the observer can go to the street, the neighbourhood, the newspaper and look. Indeed, in the same article Park gives the researcher lists of questions; for example, in a neighbourhood, what is news, where does attention generally go, and which models are imitated? Two of those questions are about communication: news and attention are the signs of a community’s common world.

The same 1915 article also brings immigrants, social mobility, public opinion and the press into a single frame; so here “the city” is not only buildings but the way people tell each other things. That is why the word “communication” in the lesson title is not a later addition: at least in Park’s hands, urban sociology was communication sociology from the start.

Key concepts

Card

Glossary

  • Chicago School: the community of sociologists who, between 1915 and 1935 at the University of Chicago, did empirical research on the city, migration and social order.
  • Human ecology: applying to human relations the way of looking used for plant and animal communities; competition creates order, and the cultural order limits that competition.
  • Social laboratory: seeing the city as the place where social processes can be observed enlarged.
  • Thomas theorem: if people define a situation as real, it is real in its consequences.
  • Urbanism: for Wirth, the way of life peculiar to the settlement of a relatively large, dense, permanent and socially heterogeneous population.
  • Immigrant press: newspapers and magazines that immigrants publish in their own languages; Park’s subject.
  • News as a form of knowledge: Park’s idea that news is a transient, talk-provoking kind of knowledge with a place of its own on the continuum between acquaintance and formal knowledge.
  • Public opinion: for Park, the shared opinion that in the city rests on the information the newspaper supplies.

Most of this glossary comes alive with an example in the cards that follow.

Story

The warden of Dannemora and one sentence

The year is 1928. William I. Thomas and Dorothy Swaine Thomas, in the methods chapter of a thick book on child behaviour (The Child in America), give an extreme example: the warden of Dannemora prison refused to carry out a court order to let an inmate out for a time, because the man was too dangerous. The inmate had killed several people who talked to themselves in the street; from the movement of their lips he imagined they were insulting him and behaved accordingly.

Right after it comes this sentence: if men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences. Robert K. Merton later named the sentence the “Thomas theorem” and counted it a theorem basic to the social sciences.

The point is this: in reality nobody was insulting him. But what determined his behaviour was not the “objective” situation but how he defined it. That is why the Thomases argued that the researcher must record not only verifiable conditions but also how the person sees those conditions, because behaviour often depends on how the person defines the situation.

What it means for a communication course: rumours, headlines and conversation are the material through which people define a “situation”. Their truth matters, but so does how people come to believe them.

Deep dive

Deep dive: Merton’s bank parable and “Thomas before Thomas”

In 1948 Merton brings the theorem to life with an explicit parable. The year is 1932; the Last National Bank is a healthy bank, and its president, Cartwright Millingville, is proud of it. One morning, as he enters, he sees some two dozen men lined up at the tellers’ cages; the queue keeps growing. For Merton this is “a situation defined as real”: once enough depositors believe a rumour that the bank will fail, the rumour brings about the failure of the bank.

Note: this is not a particular bank from history but a fictional story that Merton himself calls a “sociological parable”; the bank and its president are invented. From the parable Merton draws a general conclusion: public definitions of a situation (prophecies, predictions) become an integral part of the situation and so affect later developments. This is peculiar to human affairs: a prediction of the return of Halley’s comet does not change the comet’s orbit, but a rumour that a bank will fail can produce the failure.

A second interesting detail: Merton also writes that essentially the same theorem had been set out repeatedly, long before Thomas, by disciplined and observant minds; he names Bossuet, Mandeville, Marx and Freud. Our interpretation: the theorem’s fame may come less from the novelty of the idea than from its fitting into one short sentence and getting a name.

Our interpretation, too: the channel through which a rumour spreads (newspaper, telephone, conversation) is the communication side of the parable; the channel decides how many people the “definition of the situation” will reach.

Assumptions

Card

The assumption: what is common becomes common through communication

The Chicago School’s idea of communication came from the philosopher John Dewey. In 1916 Dewey wrote that society not only continues to exist by transmission and communication; it “may fairly be said to exist in transmission, in communication”. Then a word play: between common, community and communication there is more than a verbal tie; people live in a community by virtue of the things they have in common, and the way they come to have things in common is communication. (In Turkish the play does not survive: the words for “common”, “community” and “communication” are not related.)

In 1921 Park and Burgess put this passage into their textbook and treated communication “as Dewey has defined the term”. In 1927 Dewey carried the same idea into politics: unless the “Great Society”, bound together by industry and technology, is converted into a “Great Community”, the public remains in eclipse, and only communication can create a Great Community.

At Michigan, Charles Horton Cooley in 1909 likewise defined communication as the mechanism through which human relations exist and develop, counting among it speech and writing but also printing, railways, telegraphs and telephones (this lesson only points to him; Cooley belongs to another lesson).

Our reading: this is a relative of what would later be called the ritual view. Communication is not only carrying information but building a common world; more in the The Ritual View of Communication: Carey lesson.

Myth check

Myth: “The immigrant newspaper keeps the immigrant foreign”

What people assume. An immigrant who reads a paper in his own language never learns about the new country; the immigrant press is a kind of “separate state”. In the years of the First World War this suspicion turned into legal control: an act of 6 October 1917 required that English translations of foreign-language items about the war and the government be filed with the postmaster.

What the evidence says. Park’s 1922 book asks the question as “brake or accelerator?” The editor of the Lithuanian paper Draugas argues that the foreign-language press separates communities and prevents assimilation; other editors say the paper helps immigrants, especially the first generation, to find their bearings in the American environment and to share in its intellectual, political and social life.

Park’s own conclusion: it depends on content. Depending on what it prints, the press can hasten or retard assimilation; it can turn the immigrant’s interest back towards the home country, but it can also introduce America to him and give him the material on which to build interest and affection. So the press is not in itself a “good” or “bad” factor; it is a bridge or a wall depending on what it says.

A note on limits (our assessment): Park reports this debate through editors’ views, surveys and observation; it is a descriptive study, not a numerical test of effects. Expecting a firm measurement of “brake” or “accelerator” from it would be a mistake; but it is a good warning against blaming the language itself.

Deep dive

Deep dive: Dewey, Park and the newspaper that never appeared

The link between Dewey and Park is not only a matter of books. Park was Dewey’s student at Michigan; Dewey introduced him to the journalist Franklin Ford, and Park and Ford planned a newspaper that would make public opinion into news: Thought News. The paper was never published.

A subtlety: Dewey was at Chicago from 1894 to 1904, headed the philosophy department and founded the Laboratory School; Park came to Chicago in 1914. So they did not share a corridor in Chicago; their tie was personal and intellectual.

Dewey’s The Public and Its Problems (1927) also says something about news: news is what has just happened and is new because it departs from the old and regular; but its meaning depends on what it imports, on its social consequences. That sounds familiar next to Park’s 1940 idea that news deals with events bringing sudden and decisive change (our observation).

In the same book Dewey openly records his debt to Walter Lippmann: he acknowledges ideas from Public Opinion and The Phantom Public. Lippmann’s Public Opinion (1922), with Lippmann’s idea of “the pictures in our heads”, is also the starting point of the Agenda-Setting lesson. So Chicago’s thinking on communication meets the later debates on public opinion and the agenda at the same knot.

How it works, step by step

Step by step

How does news integrate? Five steps

If we gather Park’s writings on news into one mechanism, a chain like this emerges (the sequence is our summary):

  1. The small world. Village life rests on face-to-face relations: speech and neighbourly gossip. In the village, “everyone knows everything about everyone else.”
  2. The city and the new environment. In the industrial city the immigrant is suddenly in a wide, unfamiliar environment. The urgent information used for adjusting in that environment is called “news”.
  3. The newspaper as common ground. The newspaper is the great medium of communication within the city; public opinion rests on the information it supplies. The modern newspaper is a product of city life.
  4. Events are selected and talked about. News is the kind of event that will make people talk; it is usually limited to events bringing sudden and decisive change.
  5. Shared understanding and participation. News circulates as far as its message is understood and seems important; the more it circulates, the more the members of a society take part in common political action.

In short: in Park’s lens news makes a “bond”, not an “effect”; it is the mechanism that creates the city society’s common attention and common talk (our summary).

Card

Acquaintance or knowledge about? News is neither

Is knowing a friend the same as having knowledge about her? The philosopher and psychologist William James separated the two in 1890: to be acquainted with a thing (acquaintance with) is to be limited to the bare impression it makes; knowledge about it (knowledge about) is to know its relations. Our example: knowing the taste of a soup is one thing; knowing which kitchen it comes from and how it is made is another.

In 1940 Park applied James’s distinction to news. For him “knowledge about” is formal knowledge; “acquaintance” is unsystematic, intuitive knowledge close to common sense. These form a continuum, and news has a place of its own on it: a transient, quickly ageing kind of knowledge. News is the kind that “will make people talk”, it is usually limited to events that bring sudden and decisive change, and its function is to orient people and society in an actual world.

Why does this matter? Because news is different both from a history book’s lasting account of the world and from a neighbour’s knowing you: it is the knowledge that tells us where the world stands this morning. The same knowledge tomorrow is “old”. Chicago’s thinking on communication stands out for taking news seriously as such a concrete and transient kind of knowledge.

Step by step

Reading the city as an ecosystem: Burgess’s rings

The idea of human ecology works like this. For Park the kind of analysis used for plant and animal communities can be applied to human relations too; competition creates order, and the cultural order limits that competition on economic, political and moral levels. Applied to a city:

  1. Competition divides space. People and functions compete for room in the city.
  2. Culture limits competition. The contest never runs without rules; customs, laws and morals shape its form.
  3. Rings appear. In The City (1925) Ernest Burgess drew a city’s growth as rings expanding outward from the central business district; the model came from observing Chicago.
  4. Communication settles too. Wirth’s 1938 article lists, among the elements that explain the dominance of cities, the press, radio stations, theatres, libraries and publishing centres; so media gather where population concentrates.

Do not memorise the rings: the model was later criticised (see the critique card). The communication side of the lesson is this: a city is not just a place but a density of communication; media and news are part of the ecological order.

Lab

Lab 1: read a documented immigrant-press case

Besides Park’s book we have another heap of documents compiled from Chicago’s own newspapers: the Chicago Foreign Language Press Survey. It was set up in 1936 as an Illinois Works Progress Administration project sponsored by the Chicago Public Library; it produced about 120,000 typewritten pages of translations from Chicago’s foreign-language papers, was ended in 1941 and published in 1942. Workers selected articles by subject codes, translated and classified them; editors reviewed the translations. The Newberry Library later digitized the archive. The project’s question was how Chicago came to develop as it did, especially assimilation and cultural diffusion.

Exercise (a reading exercise). If you can reach the Newberry Library’s online archive, pick one article and ask four questions: (1) Whom is the piece addressing? (2) Does it give the reader urgent information for the new city and new job, in Park’s sense? (3) What is there about the old country? (4) Can you conclude from this one piece that “the press speeds up” or “slows down” assimilation?

The answer to the fourth question is no: one example is not evidence. Also, the pieces are a selection: the project chose by predetermined subject codes, and the pieces are translations. Before using a source as evidence, asking who chose what and why is the basic habit of any archive work.

Lab

Lab 2 (exercise): map your neighbourhood’s information channels

This is an exercise: it collects no data but builds a habit of observation. It is inspired by the list of questions in Park’s 1915 article: “What is news in this neighbourhood? Where does attention generally go?”

Choose an area of your own neighbourhood (or dormitory, or village) and, in twenty minutes, sketch this:

  1. Count the channels. Who tells whom, and how? Notice boards, shopkeepers, school, associations, places of worship, a neighbours’ group, a local page, the headman’s or caretaker’s announcements, a messaging group… write your own list.
  2. Whom does each reach? Note whom each channel reaches and whom it leaves out (language, age, occupation, computer access).
  3. What kind of information flows? Urgent information (a water cut), chatter, an official notice?
  4. Look for gaps. Which resident might get news from no channel at all?
  5. Write a one-sentence thesis: “In this neighbourhood, news travels like this…”

Warning: this exercise generates a hypothesis, not evidence. Do not write down other people’s personal information; do not copy the content of group messages, only note the type of channel and whom it reaches. Researchers in Chicago also marked on maps the places where particular behaviours occurred; spot maps and statistics were among the school’s tools.

Evidence and studies

Card

Evidence: Park’s press study and the test of the war years

The Immigrant Press and Its Control (1922) was part of a series called “Americanization Studies”, funded by the Carnegie Corporation; according to the foreword, the material had been gathered by the series’ immigrant-press division. So Park did not work alone at a desk but with the material of a research bureau.

One striking part of the book covers the war years. In Park’s account the first serious public attempt to control the immigrant press was made in the First World War, as part of the fight against enemy propaganda. Drawing on hearing records in the Post Office Department’s files, Park writes that the number of foreign-language papers actually denied Post Office services was very small, “probably not more than ten”; some newspaper offices were raided by the Department of Justice, but the Post Office Department suppressed no paper. Moreover, between 5 October 1917 and 14 January 1919, 650 foreign-language papers were given permits to publish without filing translations.

How strong is this evidence? Our assessment: it is a description based on official records; it shows what the state did, not how the press affected its readers. On the press’s effect Park is content with editors’ statements and surveys like Villchur’s. So Chicago’s strength lies less in causal claims tested with numbers than in describing “where, who and how?” with documents; it should be judged not as “right” or “wrong” but by which question it answers.

Deep dive

Deep dive: the methodological note of The Polish Peasant and the “definition of the situation”

Ten years before the theorem appeared in 1928, the first volume of Thomas and Znaniecki’s The Polish Peasant in Europe and America, published in 1918, contained a methodological note. The note says that to understand a social act one must look at three kinds of data: (1) the objective conditions under which the individual or society acts, that is, the totality of values that at the given moment affect, directly or indirectly, the conscious status of the individual or group; (2) the pre-existing attitudes of the individual or group; (3) the definition of the situation, the more or less clear conception of the conditions and of the attitudes.

The authors call the definition of the situation a “necessary preliminary to any act of the will”: in given conditions and with given attitudes an indefinite plurality of actions is possible, and a definite action appears only when conditions are selected, interpreted and combined. Usually a process of reflection occurs: either a ready social definition is applied or a new personal definition is worked out.

Our interpretation: the famous sentence of 1928 can be read as the short form of this methodological thought; the definition is the condition of behaviour. Also, we read only the first volume; the other volumes of the work (for example the one devoted to an immigrant’s life record) are not described here. This card exists to show that behind the one-sentence form of the theory stands a methodological programme.

Criticism and limits

Card

Criticism: the rings, urbanism and the language of the time

Three criticisms of the Chicago School are often repeated.

  1. The model is too simple and too American. Burgess’s rings were developed for American cities; they do not fit other settings, for example pre-industrial European cities where the centre had high status; and the separation of workplace from residence became general only in the twentieth century.
  2. Wirth’s urbanism and Fischer. In 1975 Claude Fischer wrote that Wirth’s theory had been eclipsed by a view that denies ecological factors, but that this view fails to explain the “unconventionality” of urban life. On his own model, population concentration leads to a diversity of subcultures, strengthens them and fosters diffusion among them. Fischer does not throw Wirth away: he brings the variable of size back in a different form.
  3. The language of the time. Park’s book appeared in a series called “Americanization Studies”, and assimilation was for him both an observation and a goal. Our reading: when assimilation is framed as a one-way goal there is a risk of treating the immigrant’s language and identity as an obstacle. Park’s text partly resists that risk: the immigrant’s language and memories are part of his personality, not baggage to be lost on the way; assimilation comes more readily when there are no mental conflicts.

The defenders’ reply? For this lesson we have not been able to verify a defence; in these sources there are only the criticisms. An honest conclusion: use Chicago’s concepts (ecology, urbanism, assimilation) in their historical context, knowing their limits.

Deep dive

Deep dive: Wirth’s worry about the media — one school, two moods

In 1938 Louis Wirth defined the city as “a relatively large, dense, and permanent settlement of socially heterogeneous individuals”. The same article also touches on the media. It writes that schools, movies, radio and newspapers, because they address a mass clientele, act as “leveling” influences; that the masses of the city can be steered with symbols and stereotypes by people who control the instruments of communication from afar or behind the scenes; and that in these conditions self-government can become “a mere figure of speech”.

Our reading: this tone sets an interesting contrast with Park’s comparatively hopeful language of 1915, in which the newspaper is the basis of public opinion. But Park was not naive either: in his 1938 article he thinks it probable that the cinema is more devastating to local cultures than news proper. So Chicago’s thinking on communication is neither pure optimism nor pure worry.

Our observation: Wirth’s tone comes close to the mass-society debate you will meet in the Mass Society and Mass Culture Theory lesson: a large, anonymous population and symbols managed from afar. But Wirth’s worry is a by-product of an analysis of urbanism, not the conclusion of a theory; it is safer to treat them as two separate traditions.

Deep dive

Deep dive: “Chicago or Columbia?” a crude dichotomy

A common dichotomy is told: Chicago is fieldwork and qualitative, Columbia is surveys and quantitative. There is a true core in it, but it is crude.

The core. At Columbia, around Paul Lazarsfeld and Robert Merton, survey design and “middle-range theories” developed; Samuel Stouffer had led The American Soldier study, and Lazarsfeld had set up Columbia’s office of radio research. Herbert Blumer, trained at Chicago, published in 1956 an article criticising “variable analysis”.

The crudeness. (a) Chicago was not only qualitative: according to Bulmer, a variety of qualitative and quantitative methods were pioneered in 1915–1935. (b) Lazarsfeld was not only quantitative: Jeřábek writes that in the literature he is often regarded as a purely quantitative methodologist, whereas he combined quantitative and qualitative methods; reason analysis, which asks “why?”, begins qualitative and ends quantitative.

The real loss. According to Wahl-Jorgensen, Chicago’s earliest communication programmes were short-lived interdisciplinary committees that died for lack of institutional support; they are unknown in the field’s history today. Another piece by the same author tells two stories: a tale of triumph (Chicago’s normative and empirical influence on media research) and a tale of loss (the decline of a socially engaged, often qualitative approach). Both are true.

Our inference: the rivalry was between people and institutions, not between methods. “Which method for which question?” is a more productive question than “who is right?”

Today: digital and social media

Card

Today: communication infrastructure and the “storytelling neighbourhood”

Park’s immigrant-press question is alive today. In 2001 Ball-Rokeach, Kim and Matei studied seven residential areas in Los Angeles, in old and new immigrant communities, with multilingual data collection, and asked: what turns a person from the occupant of a house into a member of a neighbourhood? What they found: the most important factor in belonging was an active and integrated “storytelling system” that connected residents, community organizations and local media.

This can be read as an indirect descendant of Park’s idea (our interpretation): community is built by shared news and shared narrative. But two cautions. First, the study is from 2001; its abstract mentions neither today’s messaging groups nor social media. So saying “a group chat is a storytelling system” rests not on evidence but on our own reasoning. Second, the finding should not be read simply as “the more local media, the more belonging”: what is stressed is that the system is integrated and active.

We can extend the same reasoning to the Thomas theorem (again our own reasoning): a rumour or a headline produces consequences once enough people take it to be real; a neighbourhood group is a channel that can carry a rumour fast. Chicago’s two ideas, that shared information builds community and that definitions shape behaviour, feed each other in today’s networks, too.

Deep dive

Deep dive: traces from Chicago — Blumer as a pointer

One way to trace Chicago’s communication legacy runs through Herbert Blumer. Blumer took his doctorate at Chicago in 1928, and in 1937, in an overview article on social psychology in the volume Man and Society edited by W. Schmidt, he coined the term “symbolic interaction”. At Chicago he is said to have been influenced by the thought of George Herbert Mead. Symbolic interactionism will be treated separately in the Functionalism and Symbolic Interactionism lesson; here we only leave a pointer.

Blumer also did media research: Movies and Conduct (1933) is one of the Payne Fund studies. The story of those studies is in the The Payne Fund Studies and the Invasion from Mars lesson.

Wahl-Jorgensen’s summary fits here: the Chicago School subtly but profoundly shaped the direction of scholarship on the media, but the history of its engagement with mass communication researchers is also a history of missed connections and opportunities. So Chicago’s legacy should be read as a story of triumph and a story of loss together; to say that a school either still goes on or has wholly ended would leave both out.

Typical question patterns

  • “Which sociologist studied the immigrant press and treated news as a form of knowledge?” → Robert E. Park (The Immigrant Press and Its Control, 1922; “News as a Form of Knowledge”, 1940).
  • “Whose sentence is ‘If men define situations as real…’?” → W. I. Thomas (with Dorothy Swaine Thomas, 1928); the name is Merton’s.
  • “Whose is the concentric-ring model?” → Ernest Burgess (1925).
  • “Urbanism as a way of life?” → Louis Wirth (1938).
  • “The link between communication and community?” → John Dewey (1916, 1927).

Tempting wrong answers

  • Attributing The Polish Peasant to Park: the work is by Thomas and Znaniecki.
  • Reading the Thomas theorem as “if people believe something false, the outcome is false”: the theorem says the consequences are real even if the belief is false.
  • Treating “Chicago = qualitative, Columbia = quantitative” as fixed: see the nuances in the deep card.
  • Thinking Park was born an academic: he spent 1887–1898 as a journalist.

Summary

Summary card: one sentence from Chicago

  • Context: fast-growing Chicago full of immigrants; the first sociology department in 1892; 1915–1935 the “Chicago School”.
  • Core claim: society exists in communication (Dewey); communication integrates, competition separates (Park).
  • City = laboratory (Park 1915): fieldwork, maps, letters, newspapers.
  • The immigrant press: brake or accelerator? Park: it depends on content. In wartime, a translation requirement; in the Post Office records only very few papers were actually denied service.
  • News is a form of knowledge (Park 1940): on the continuum between acquaintance and formal knowledge, a transient, talk-provoking, orienting kind of knowledge (from James’s distinction).
  • The Thomas theorem (1928): if you define situations as real, they are real in their consequences; the belief need not be true. Merton named it; the bank parable is fiction.
  • Human ecology and Burgess’s rings: the city shaped by competition; criticism: specific to American cities, too simple.
  • Wirth: the city = a large, dense, permanent, differentiated population; he also carried a worry about the media.
  • Today: the “storytelling system” (Ball-Rokeach et al. 2001); for social media, only our own reasoning.
  • Exam traps: Park–Thomas (immigrant press or letters?); the “Chicago = qualitative” shortcut.
