Concept

replication crisis

The finding that many published results do not hold up when studies are repeated, and the debate about causes such as flexible analysis and selective reporting.

The replication crisis is the finding that a substantial share of published studies do not give the same result when repeated, and the debate about why. The Open Science Collaboration (2015) repeated 100 studies from three psychology journals: 97 percent of the original studies and 36 percent of the replications gave significant results, and replication effects were about half the size of the originals.

One cause is questionable research practices: Simmons and colleagues showed that flexibility in data collection and analysis raises false-positive rates dramatically; a survey of more than 2,000 psychologists by John and colleagues found these practices surprisingly common; and Matthes and colleagues found similar signs in communication experiments. Proposed remedies include preregistration (writing the question and analysis plan before seeing results). Failing to replicate is not, by itself, fraud. See meta-analysis, effect size.

Example

A 20-person experiment showing that “listening to a song made people younger”, made significant through flexible analysis.
