Glossary of concepts

Short, sourced definitions of the concepts used in the course, with the lessons where they appear.

  • administrative research — In Lazarsfeld’s 1941 distinction, communication research with empirical methods that serves the practical aims of a particular institution.
  • agenda-setting — The idea that the emphasis the news media give to issues influences how important the public considers those issues: the media shape not what people think, but what they think about.
  • climate of opinion — The perception of which opinion is widespread, rising or in favour around one on an issue. In the spiral of silence, this perception rather than the real distribution shapes behaviour.
  • communication — The process in which people try to convey or share meaning through symbols. It has no single definition: some definitions stress the transmission of a message, others the building of shared meaning.
  • critical research — In Lazarsfeld’s 1941 distinction, communication research that questions, on the basis of values, the media’s general role in society and the problems beneath it.
  • cultivation theory — Gerbner’s theory that heavy television viewers’ perception of social reality is closer to the recurring messages of the on-screen world than that of similar light viewers; it sees the effect as accumulation spread over years.
  • Cultural Indicators Project — A long-running research programme that George Gerbner started in the late 1960s, monitoring television content over the years and combining it with viewer surveys; the framework of cultivation theory.
  • falsifiability — That a theory could turn out incompatible with some possible observation; for Popper, the criterion separating scientific from non-scientific theory.
  • fear of isolation — Fear of being excluded, of falling out of one’s surroundings’ favour. In the spiral of silence, the engine that drives people to probe the climate of opinion and to fall silent when they sense they are in the minority.
  • gatekeeping — The selection process that determines whether a story or piece of information passes through a media channel; the first link in the formation of the media agenda.
  • hard core — The minority that is not caught in the spiral and goes on defending its opinion, accepting exclusion (or because it is already excluded). Noelle-Neumann adds the “avant-garde” beside it.
  • heavy viewers — The upper end of the grouping by amount of television viewing in cultivation research; on the other side of the comparison are light viewers (those who watch little).
  • mainstreaming — The way heavy television viewing erodes differences in outlook that stem from factors such as age, education and political orientation and brings people closer to a common “television mainstream”.
  • mass communication — A form of communication in which messages reach a dispersed audience through technical means, publicly, indirectly and one-way; the communicator is usually an institution.
  • mean world syndrome — Heavy television viewers seeing the world as more dangerous, insecure and mean than it really is; the best-known concept of Gerbner’s cultivation work.
  • media agenda — The ordering of issues by importance in newspapers, bulletins and websites: which issue gets how much space, airtime and priority.
  • media-centric — A view that grants media and communication autonomy and influence of their own and treats media as a driver of social change (McQuail).
  • message system analysis — The second prong of Cultural Indicators: systematically counting and tracking recurring patterns in media content (who appears, what they do, what they do to whom).
  • metacommunication — Communication about communication: cues about how a message is to be understood and how the two parties relate. For Watzlawick, the relationship aspect of communication classifies its content aspect.
  • middle-range theory — A testable theory of a delimited domain, standing between small working hypotheses and large theories that aim to explain everything at once.
  • need for orientation — A concept that predicts a person’s wish to turn to the news media on an issue: the combination of personal relevance and uncertainty. It explains in whom agenda-setting will be stronger.
  • nonverbal communication — Communication through everything other than words, such as facial expression, gesture, posture, tone of voice and distance; it works together with verbal communication and often complements it.
  • paradigm — In Kuhn, the exemplary achievements a scientific community shares, together with the beliefs, values and tools that go with them; in communication studies often used loosely for a “dominant approach”.
  • priming — An issue or area that stands out in the media becoming the yardstick people use when judging a leader or an event.
  • public agenda — The ranking of the issues that the public (or a sample) considers important; usually measured by survey.
  • public opinion — In the spiral of silence, public opinion is not a voting total but a means of social control: the opinions a person can express openly without being excluded, or must openly show in order not to be excluded.
  • quasi-statistical sense — According to Noelle-Neumann, an almost statistician-like “sixth sense” that lets people, without running a poll, sense which opinion is rising and which is declining around them.
  • resonance — When everyday life experience and television messages overlap, the cultivation effect gets a “double dose” and becomes stronger.
  • ritual view — The view that sees communication less as carrying information and more as sharing, participation and community, focused on representing and maintaining shared beliefs. It is the view James W. Carey proposed against the transmission view.
  • salience — How far an issue, person or attribute stands out on an agenda; that is, its perceived importance. In agenda-setting this is what is transferred from the media to the public.
  • second-level agenda-setting — The idea that the media transfer to the public not only which issues, but also which attributes of issues or persons stand out.
  • society-centric — A view that sees the media mainly as a reflection of political and economic forces and media theory as a special application of broader social theory (McQuail).
  • spiral of silence — People sense which opinion is gaining strength around them; those left in the minority fall silent out of fear of isolation, which makes their opinion look weaker than it is, and the process accelerates like a spiral. Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann’s theory.
  • theory — A systematic set of ideas that helps make sense of a phenomenon, explain it, predict a consequence and guide action; in the strict sense, connected propositions from which observed regularities can be derived.
  • transmission view — The view that sees communication as the sending of messages over distance, the carrying of information from one place to another. One of the two views that Carey contrasts with each other.
