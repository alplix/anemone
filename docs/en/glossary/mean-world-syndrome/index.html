Concept

mean world syndrome

Heavy television viewers seeing the world as more dangerous, insecure and mean than it really is; the best-known concept of Gerbner’s cultivation work.

The mean world syndrome is the best-known concept to come out of Gerbner’s cultivation work: heavy television viewers see the world as more dangerous, insecure and mean than it really is. Britannica defines it as believing that the world is more violent and mean than it actually is.

This is not the claim that “people who watch violence commit violence”. Gerbner used to say that the direct contribution to violence is negligible; what really worried him was that frightened people are more dependent and more easily steered. In surveys the concept shows up like this: to questions such as “Would you be afraid to walk in your neighbourhood at night?” heavy viewers more often answer “yes”. Gerbner described this as the internalization of a sense of danger, mistrust and meanness.

In Turkish, renderings such as “kötü dünya sendromu” (“bad world syndrome”; e.g. Özkan 2017) and “acımasız dünya sendromu” (“merciless world syndrome”) circulate. The concept rests on a finding of association; critics such as Doob and Macdonald showed that the viewing–fear relationship disappears when the actual crime rate is controlled. That is why causality is disputed. For details see Cultivation Theory and resonance.

Example

The proportion answering “yes” to the question about being afraid to walk alone on campus or in the neighbourhood at night coming out higher among heavy viewers than among light viewers.
