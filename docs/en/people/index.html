People behind the theories

Scholars, philosophers and researchers who appear in the course: who they were, what they did and where to meet them in the lessons.

  • Benjamin Day

    American printer and newspaper publisher; in 1833 he started the penny press by launching the New York Sun at one cent.

  • Bernard Cohen

    American political scientist; the sentence about “what to think about” in The Press and Foreign Policy (1963) became the slogan of agenda-setting theory.

  • Carroll J. Glynn

    Communication researcher working on public opinion and social norms. In 1997, with Andrew Hayes and James Shanahan, wrote the meta-analysis on the spiral of silence.

  • Claude E. Shannon

    American mathematician and engineer (1916–2001). At Bell Laboratories he wrote the 1948 paper that founded information theory, with the bit and the boxed diagram of a communication system.

  • Denis McQuail

    British communication theorist (1935–2017); author of the textbook “Mass Communication Theory”.

  • Donald Shaw

    Founder of agenda-setting research with Maxwell McCombs; professor of journalism and press historian at Chapel Hill (d. 2021).

  • Elisabeth Noelle-Neumann

    German public opinion researcher and communication scholar (1916–2010). Founder of the Allensbach Institute; developer of the spiral of silence theory.

  • George Gerbner

    Hungarian-born American communication researcher (1919–2005); dean of the Annenberg School. Ran the Cultural Indicators project; known for cultivation theory and the “mean world syndrome”.

  • Hadley Cantril

    American psychologist and public opinion researcher, known for The Invasion from Mars (1940) on Orson Welles’s 1938 radio play. Textbooks often present the book as a “magic bullet” example.

  • Harold D. Lasswell

    American political scientist (1902–1978). His book on war propaganda appeared in 1927; in 1948 he described communication with five questions and three social functions.

  • İbrahim Müteferrika

    Ottoman official of Transylvanian origin; in 1727 he received imperial permission for a Muslim-run press, and his first book appeared in 1729.

  • James W. Carey

    American communication theorist and journalism educator (d. 2006). Known for Communication as Culture (1989), which proposed looking at communication through two lenses, “transmission” and “ritual”.

  • Johannes Gutenberg

    Goldsmith-printer from Mainz, remembered as the pioneer of printing with movable metal type in Europe, though surprisingly little is documented about his life.

  • Karl Popper

    Vienna-born philosopher of science (1902–1994), known for the criterion that a scientific theory must be falsifiable.

  • Larry Gross

    American communication researcher. Directed Cultural Indicators with Gerbner at the University of Pennsylvania; co-author of the 1976 Violence Profile article. Later a school director at USC.

  • Maxwell McCombs

    One of the two founders of agenda-setting research; did the 1968 Chapel Hill study with Donald Shaw and developed need for orientation and second-level agenda-setting.

  • Michael Morgan

    US communication researcher; emeritus professor at UMass Amherst. Worked with Gerbner, co-author of the 1980 mainstreaming article; wrote cultivation meta-analyses and state-of-the-field reports.

  • Nancy Signorielli

    American communication researcher; professor at the University of Delaware. Original member of the Cultural Indicators team; worked on television characters and cultivation analysis.

  • Paul Lazarsfeld

    Vienna-born American sociologist; directed the Bureau of Applied Social Research at Columbia and, with his voter studies and the two-step flow idea, became the emblem of the limited-effects era.

  • Paul M. Hirsch

    American sociologist and organization researcher. In 1980–81 he reanalysed the Gerbner team’s cultivation findings and questioned the theory’s empirical basis.

  • Paul Watzlawick

    Austrian-born American psychologist and communication theorist (1921–2007). Worked at the Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto; known for the five axioms that include “one cannot not communicate”.

  • Robert K. Merton

    American sociologist (1910–2003); the name behind “middle-range theory”, established terms such as the self-fulfilling prophecy and the focus group, and the Columbia research tradition.

  • Robert T. Craig

    American communication theorist; mapped communication theory as “seven traditions” in a 1999 article.

  • Solomon Asch

    Polish-born American social psychologist (1907–1996). Known for his 1950s line-length conformity experiments; in Noelle-Neumann’s theory, one of the psychological supports of fear of isolation.

  • Thomas S. Kuhn

    American historian and philosopher of science (1922–1996); turned “paradigm” into a central idea for how science works.

  • W. James Potter

    American communication researcher; emeritus professor at UC Santa Barbara. Expert in media effects and media literacy; criticized cultivation theory conceptually in 1993 and 2014.

  • Walter Lippmann

    American journalist and commentator; with the ideas of “the pictures in our heads” and the “pseudo-environment” in Public Opinion (1922) he laid the intellectual ground of agenda-setting theory.

  • Warren Weaver

    American mathematician and science administrator (1894–1978). He ran the Rockefeller Foundation’s natural sciences programme and wrote the essay that opened Shannon’s theory to general readers.
