Émile Durkheim

French sociologist (1858–1917); known for social facts, mechanical and organic solidarity, anomie and his study of suicide; one of the founders of sociology.

  • 1858–1917
  • Born: Épinal
  • sociologist

Biography

Émile Durkheim was born in 1858 in Épinal, in the Lorraine region, into a family of rabbis: his father, grandfather and great-grandfather were all rabbis. He entered the École normale supérieure in 1879 and studied philosophy. In 1887 he began teaching the first official sociology courses in France at the University of Bordeaux. In 1902 he moved to the Sorbonne, to the chair of the Science of Education, which was renamed “Science of Education and Sociology” in 1913.

During his Bordeaux years he published three books: The Division of Labour in Society (1893), The Rules of Sociological Method (1895) and Suicide (1897); in 1896 he founded L’année sociologique. His last major work, The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, appeared in 1912. He argued that the subject matter of sociology is “social facts”, that is, ways of acting, thinking and feeling that exist outside the individual and exert pressure on him.

The First World War took many of his most promising students; in 1915 his son André died in combat. Durkheim died of a stroke in November 1917. He defended Dreyfus during the Dreyfus affair and was a founding member of the Human Rights League.
