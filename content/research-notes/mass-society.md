Author: Claude (lesson mass-society; people tocqueville, le-bon, tarde, ortega, kornhauser, mannheim; concepts mass-society, crowd-psychology, public-vs-crowd, tyranny-of-the-majority, atomisation, mass-culture-fear; bibliography `content/bibliography/mass-society.json`, 40+ new entries, all `verified.status: ok`; terminology `content/terminology/mass-society.json`).

Method: dates, titles, publishers and quoted sentences were checked against at least two sources I opened (primary text plus reference work, Crossref plus OpenAlex abstract, Open Library plus encyclopedia, publisher page). Primary texts actually read: Le Bon (French transcription on Wikisource and the English translation on Project Gutenberg), Tocqueville (Reeve translation, Project Gutenberg, both volumes), Tarde (chapter 1 of L'opinion et la foule in a 1910 Alcan printing on the Internet Archive; the 1903 English Laws of Imitation; the mediastudies.press excerpt with Patrick Parsons's introduction), Reicher 1984 (full text), Kornhauser's 1968 encyclopedia entry (whole entry), Hamilton's and Marshall's entries (whole entries), the UC In Memoriam notice, and two Turkish articles (Polat & Durmuş 2015; Aktürkoğlu 2021). Everything else was read at abstract or publisher-description level, and each bibliography `note` says so.

## (a) Could not verify, therefore left out

- **Date of the English translation of The Crowd**: Open Library and Wikipedia give 1896, Encyclopedia.com gives 1897. Not stated in the lesson (only the French 1895 date).
- **Freud's Group Psychology (1921)** and any Le Bon–Freud link: Encyclopedia.com says only that Freud and Allport acknowledged his importance; the 1921 book is not named.
- **Le Bon's birth and death places**: places differ between sources; only years used.

Tocqueville
- **His shift to pessimism after 1848** (Kornhauser's entry): one source. Left out.
- **Birthplace** (Paris in one entry, elsewhere Verneuil): left out.

Kornhauser, Mannheim, Blumer, Bell, Bramson, Shils
- **The 1959 book was not read.** The formula (accessible elites, available masses) and the three intellectual streams come from Kornhauser's own 1968 entry, which cites the book. A search-summary quotation of the book's own wording was not used because its page was not opened.
- **Mannheim** was not read at all; he appears only as Kornhauser and the encyclopedias place him. His Ideology and Utopia and its year were not verified and are left out. The two encyclopedia entries differ on where he studied (Heidelberg under Weber versus Freiburg), so his bio says only Berlin and Paris, then teaching at Heidelberg and Frankfurt.
- **Blumer's definition of "mass"** (anonymous, heterogeneous, little interaction): not verified in an opened source; Blumer appears only as Kornhauser and Parsons mention him.
- **Bell (1956, reprinted 2004) and Bramson (1961, chapter five)**: only Crossref and Open Library records; the texts were not read. The lesson says so.
- **Shils, "Daydreams and Nightmares" (1957) and "Mass Society and Its Culture" (1960)**: I could not match the 1957 article in Crossref; not cited. Shils's role is mentioned only through Pooley 2006 in the existing bibliography (not in this lesson's text).
- **Kornhauser's "aristocratic" versus "democratic" wording** is his own; Hamilton's right/neutral/left is a different scheme. Both are given and attributed.

## (b) Uncertainties that remain in the text

- Placing **Le Bon in the "aristocratic" strand** is my synthesis: Kornhauser does not classify him; Hamilton names him among the leading nineteenth-century proponents "from a different perspective"; Encyclopedia.com calls him "antidemocrat and elitist".
- The bridge **"atomised audience, therefore powerful effects"** (card crit-powerful, step 2), the reading of **Berman 1997** against Kornhauser (deep-berman) and the pairing of **Reicher's identity limit with Brady et al.'s group-bounded diffusion** (today-lab, deep-viral) are the lesson's own inferences and are labelled as such ("bizim yorumumuz").
- **Hamilton** is a strong critic (he says nearly all major claims of the theory are contradicted by evidence); the lesson attributes the claims to him and sets Kornhauser's reply beside them. His and Kornhauser's judgements of the book's impact differ (UC In Memoriam: "would become a classic"; Hamilton: "little impact"); both are reported.
- **Reicher's interpretation** that crowd psychology was born as an establishment response is presented as his interpretation.
- The Tarde example about regions "where no Jew has ever been seen" is quoted from the French text because it shows his argument about the press; it is a painful example and is framed as such.
- The `hamilton-...` and `marshall-...` bibliography entries point to the same Encyclopedia.com page as Kornhauser's entry (three entries on one page); their URLs carry a `#hamilton` / `#marshall` fragment to keep them distinct. The fragments are not real anchors.
- The lesson meta timeline lists Ortega under 1930 (book); the 1929 newspaper series is mentioned in the label and in the text.
- Person and concept EN files are machine translations (status `machine`).
- Counts of words per card are sometimes below 150 (the shortest cores are 127-148 words without link markup); the deeper material sits in the deep cards.
