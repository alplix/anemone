# Research notes: lesson `chicago-school` (Chicago Okulu ve İletişim), 2026-09-20

Files: lesson text `content/i18n/{tr,en}/courses/mass-communication-theories/lessons/chicago-school.json` (20 core + 7 deep cards, 10 quiz questions, no `turkey` section); lesson meta; people `park burgess thomas wirth dewey`; concepts `chicago-school human-ecology thomas-theorem immigrant-press urbanism news-as-knowledge social-laboratory`; bibliography `content/bibliography/chicago-school.json` (55 entries, all `verified.status: ok`, each note says exactly what was read); terminology `content/terminology/chicago-school.json`. Lesson status in `course.json` was set to `ready` only temporarily for validation and reverted.

Method: every date, number and biographical detail in the text was checked against sources I opened. Where a primary text was available (Park 1915, 1922, 1923; Park & Burgess 1921; Thomas & Thomas 1928; Thomas & Znaniecki 1918 vol. I; Merton 1948; Wirth 1938; Dewey 1916 and 1927; James 1890 ch. IX; Cooley 1909) I read the passages used in full text. Full texts came from Internet Archive scans, Project Gutenberg and (for Merton 1948, Wirth 1938 and Blumer 1956) PDF copies whose text layer I had to decode by script; the Wirth text was read closely, the Blumer PDF only skimmed (part of its text layer stayed garbled) and used just for its page range and one paraphrased sentence. Everything else rests on abstracts (OpenAlex/Crossref), publisher pages and encyclopaedia entries, and is marked as such in the bibliography notes.

## (a) Could NOT verify, therefore left out

- **Park 1936, 1938, 1940 (full texts)**: only the journal abstracts were read (via OpenAlex/Crossref). The lesson therefore says only what the abstracts say. In particular the lesson does **not** state where Park puts news on the acquaintance/knowledge-about continuum (the abstract says only that news "also has a point" on it, characterised as transient and ephemeral). Textbooks often say "between the two"; I could not confirm that from the article.
- **Park's "The City as a Social Laboratory" (1929)** and the exact phrase "social laboratory" as Park's own coinage: not opened. The lesson builds "social laboratory" on Park 1915 ("laboratory or clinic", read in full text) and on Turkish reference entries that use the term; it does not date or attribute the exact phrase.
- **Park's line that the city is "a state of mind, a body of customs and traditions"**: I looked for it in the 1915 article and did not find it; it probably belongs to the 1925 book version (which I did not read). Left out.
- **Park's advice to students to "get the seat of your pants dirty in real research"**: widely repeated, but I found no source I could open that documents it; left out.
- **Burgess's own chapter "The Growth of the City"** and *The City* (1925) as a book: not read. The lesson states only that Burgess drew the city's growth as rings around the central business district (two reference pages agree) and lists no zone names; a third source (transport-geography page) lists six rings including a factory zone while others list five, so the zones are not enumerated. The named later critics (Hoyt, Harris and Ullman) appeared only in search summaries and are not named.
- **Human ecology terms "invasion", "succession", "natural area"**: the 1921 textbook uses them and "human ecology" appears in it, but I did not read those chapters closely enough to define them, so they are not in the lesson.
- **Wirth 1948, "Consensus and Mass Communication"** (ASR 13(1)): the title, journal, issue and year are confirmed by Crossref and Wikipedia; its page range was not, and the article was not read. It is mentioned once in Wirth's person text only, without a bibliography entry.
- **Morris Janowitz, *The Community Press in an Urban Setting* (1952)**: existence and publisher (Free Press, Glencoe; University of Chicago Press for the 1967 edition) seen in catalogue listings, content not read; a natural "influence on later media research" example that was dropped rather than described from memory.
- **Platt, *A History of Sociological Research Methods in America 1920-1960*** and Hammersley on Blumer: seen only as blurbs/listings; not used. The Chicago-versus-Columbia deep card therefore rests on Bulmer's publisher blurb, Goldthorpe's chapter abstract, Jeřábek 2022 (read), Wahl-Jorgensen's two abstracts and Blumer's 1956 title/header. **No source I opened states the "rivalry" as such**; the card frames it as a common narrative and corrects it with cited nuances. The "defenders' reply" to the criticisms of the ring model and of Park's assimilationism could not be sourced and the critique card says so.
- **Blumer's 1956 paper as a presidential address**: stated by Wikipedia and a search summary; the lesson only says it was a 1956 article criticising variable analysis. Blumer's move from Chicago to Berkeley (1952) was not verified in an opened source, so the lesson says "trained at Chicago", not "at Chicago".
- **Mead**: named once (Blumer card, "is said to have been influenced by") on Wikipedia's word only. Mead, Cooley, Blumer, symbolic interactionism, the definition-of-the-situation concept and the research-methods concepts belong to other lessons and are mentioned in plain text (Cooley 1909 was read only to state his definition of communication).
- **The Franklin Ford collection / Thought News details** (dates 1888-1892, the 58-page manifesto, a first issue prepared but not distributed): seen only in a search summary of a page that returned 403 to me. The lesson gives only what EBSCO and Wikipedia say: Dewey introduced Park to Ford, they planned a paper called Thought News to report public opinion, it was never published. No dates.
- **Britannica** (Park, AJS) and the Cardiff repository copy of Wahl-Jorgensen's chapter returned 403 or blocked pages; I relied on Crossref/OpenAlex abstracts instead.
- **Quotations**: none longer than 15 words. Quoted English fragments: "it may fairly be said to exist in transmission, in communication" (Dewey 1916), "a mere figure of speech" and "a relatively large, dense, and permanent settlement of socially heterogeneous individuals" (Wirth 1938), "laboratory or clinic" and "in excess" (Park 1915), "If men define situations as real, they are real in their consequences." (Thomas & Thomas 1928, p. 572, read in the 1928 text), "probably not more than ten" and "understand only one word out of five" (Park 1922), "everyone knows everything about everyone else" (Park & Burgess 1921), "something that will make people talk" (Park 1940 abstract), "as Dewey has defined the term" (Park & Burgess 1921). The long sentence "News is a kind of urgent information that men use in making adjustments..." (Park 1922, p. 9, verified) is paraphrased, not quoted.

## (b) Conflicts between sources and how the lesson handles them

- **Park's Tuskegee years**: EBSCO says 1905-1912, Wikipedia 1905-1914 ("some sources 1907-1914"). No end year given.
- **Park's Chicago tenure end**: Encyclopedia.com/IESS says 1929, Wikipedia and EBSCO 1933. No year given. His start (1914) is agreed.
- **Park's Michigan degree**: Ph.B. (IESS) vs B.A. (EBSCO, Wikipedia). The lesson says only that he studied philosophy there.
- **Park's number of Simmel courses** (one vs three): the lesson says only that he attended Simmel's lectures.
- **W. I. Thomas's Chicago years** (1894-1918 vs 1895-1918) and the date of the volumes of *The Polish Peasant* (1918-1920 vs 1918-1921): lesson uses "1918-1920" for the work and gives no start year for his Chicago post; his 1918 departure is stated without the reason (Wikipedia gives an arrest and dismissal, Encyclopedia.com a move to Americanization studies). The personal scandal was deliberately left out.
- **The Child in America (1928)**: Encyclopedia.com's Thomas entry dates it 1927 and names only W. I. Thomas; the book itself (title page and copyright page read) says 1928 and lists both authors.
- **Wirth's presidency of the International Sociological Association**: 1949-1952 (Wikipedia) vs 1950 (Encyclopedia.com); no year given.
- **Chicago Foreign Language Press Survey**: the Newberry site says 1855-1938, the University of Chicago finding aid 1861-1938; the lesson uses neither start year (only "more than twenty communities", "about 120,000 pages", 1936, 1941, 1942). The Newberry list has 23 language names for "22 communities", so the lesson does not print "22".
- **Park 1922 survey items**: the number of periodicals in Park's tables is garbled in the OCR and not used. The Villchur figures (312/16/10/12, 25 per cent) are read in the text of both Park 1922 and Park 1923 and agree; the survey's date is taken from Park's footnote to Villchur's article of 10 June 1919 and the text says "publishes the results ... in June 1919", not the date of the questionnaire.
- **Concentric zones**: see (a).
- **Wirth's definition**: in his summary section the definition omits "socially" ("relatively large, dense, and permanent settlement of heterogeneous individuals"); the fuller form (with "socially") from p. 8 is used.
- **Burgess**: birth place/date from Wikipedia (Tilbury, Ontario, 16 May 1886) are supported by Encyclopedia.com only at the level "Ontario, 1886"; the person text gives the day and town on Wikipedia's word, the year on both. The Burgess text does not name him president of the American Sociological Society (year not agreed).

## (c) Design choices worth a second look

- **The Villchur survey opens the lesson.** It is documented twice in Park's own texts, but the survey itself is Villchur's, reported by Park; I did not open Villchur's article.
- **Dziennik Zwiazkowy** is placed as a Chicago Polish daily of the Polish National Alliance via the Illinois Newspaper Project page (labour content moved there in 1908). The letter writer's own location is not stated in the letters I read, and the lesson does not say where he lived. The letters' names (a family series) are not used.
- **The "Osinski series" letters (Nos. 68-138)**: the first two items are replies to the advertisement, addressed to the authors; the lesson calls the advertisers "Thomas and Znaniecki" on that basis (the bracketed editorial note says the letters were written to the authors).
- **Merton's bank** is presented as a fictional parable in both the story card and the deep card; Merton himself calls it a "sociological parable". His "Thomas theorem" wording in the 1948 essay was read in the PDF; the lesson quotes the 1928 wording from the 1928 book.
- **Labelled interpretation**: sentences beginning "Bizim yorumumuz / okumamız / gözlemimiz" (EN: "Our interpretation / reading / observation") mark the author's own reasoning: the link between Dewey's "common-community-communication" and the later ritual view, the reading of the "social laboratory", the ecological reading of media concentration, the parable's communication side, and the social-media extension in the "today" card. The lesson does not claim Carey drew on Dewey.
- **Lab cards** are labelled as exercises. The FLPS reading exercise sends the reader to the Newberry archive but describes no specific article.
- **Names of institutions**: Chicago, Columbia and other universities appear only as historical facts about the school and the rival centre; no course, programme or exam body is named.
