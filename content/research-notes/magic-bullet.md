## (0) Corrections to the brief (read this first)

- **Berlo 1960 did not use “bullet”.** In the scanned text of *The Process of Communication* (pp. 27–28, index “hypodermic needle concept, 27–28”) the phrase is “hypodermic needle” / “hypodermic-needle”, applied to “much of the early discussion of the effects of the mass media” with no author named, as a foil for Berlo’s own process view. The word “bullet” does not occur in that sense. The **“Bullet Theory”** wording is **Schramm’s**: Schramm & Roberts 1971 (rev. ed.), Schramm’s chapter p. 8: “I have elsewhere called this the Bullet Theory of communication”; p. 10: by the middle 1950s it “had been shot full of holes”; the editors’ introduction (p. 59) and Schramm’s foreword to the second edition also use “bullet theory”. Where Schramm named it “elsewhere” (i.e. in an earlier work) I could not establish; I did not open the 1954 first edition (borrowing-only). The lesson therefore says “first *documented* users”, not “first users”.
- **Lasswell 1927 contains neither phrase.** Checked in OCR text of two scans (1927 London/New York edition, Delhi Univ. Library copy; 1938 Peter Smith reprint): no “hypodermic”, “needle”, “syringe”, “magic bullet”. Genuine near-misses are reported honestly in the lesson: “Thoughts are Bullets” (a quotation, attributed in the book to George William Curtis), a footnoted book title (*Bullets and Billets*, 1917) and “injected into the veins” (a figure of speech about Wilson’s propaganda, p. 217). OCR risk: a phrase could be hidden by an OCR error, but two independent scans agree, and the Wikipedia article on the model reports the same (weak corroboration).
- **“Transmission belt”**: I found the name only on Wikipedia and popular study sites (Simply Psychology etc.), not in any scholarly source I could open. Left out of the lesson entirely.
- **Chaffee & Hochheimer** is cited as a 1985 chapter in Rogers & Balle (eds.), *The media revolution in America and in Western Europe* (Ablex), pp. 267–296, as given by Pooley & Socolow 2013 and a search-index listing. A 1992 bibliography (ERIC ED355593) gives another venue (Gurevitch & Levy, *Mass Communication Review Yearbook* 5, pp. 75–104). I did not resolve the conflict (possibly two printings). I did not read the chapter; it is only named as one of the historians in Pooley & Socolow’s list.

## (a) Could not verify, therefore left out (or explicitly hedged)

- **Cantril’s 1.7 million / 1.2 million figures** (“believed it was news / were frightened”): appear on Wikipedia and popular pages; not found in a source I could open. The lesson uses only Cantril’s own summary sentence (“at least six million … at least a million frightened or disturbed”), which I read in the 1971 reprint and a second copy.
- **Cantril 1940 book itself** was not opened (borrowing-only copy). Everything about the study comes from (i) Cantril’s own summary (reprinted in Schramm & Roberts 1971, pp. 579–595; originally Newcomb, Hartley et al. 1947), (ii) Pooley & Socolow 2013 (full text), (iii) their Slate article. Publication month differs between sources (March per Pooley & Socolow; 15 April per SFE), so only the year is used. **Pages 579–595 are inferred from running heads.**
- **Hooper 2 %**: from Pooley & Socolow’s Slate article, which cites a summary in *Broadcasting*; I did not see that summary. Independent support for “panic greatly exaggerated” rests on the NPR review of Schwartz 2015 and on Hayes & Battles 2011 (abstract only). **I did not open Campbell, *Getting It Wrong*, or Schwartz, *Broadcast Hysteria*** (borrowing-only); their claims are reported only as relayed by Slate and NPR/Heller and are labelled that way (“Campbell’a göre…”, “Schwartz’ın kitabı … savunur”).
- **The Slate piece and the IJoC article share authors** (Pooley & Socolow); they are not independent of each other. Independent corroboration: Hayes & Battles 2011 (abstract) and Heller 2015 (review).
- **Bineham 1988, Sproule 1989, Sproule 1987, Parsons 2021, Pooley 2006, Chaffee 1988, Bennett & Iyengar 2008, Kalla & Broockman 2018, Hayes & Battles 2011, Lubken 2008**: I read Crossref/ERIC/OpenAlex abstracts (Sproule 1987’s and Parsons’s and Pooley’s abstracts in full), not the full texts (publisher pages returned 403). Lubken’s chapter: only its title, place in the volume (Library of Congress table of contents) and the way it is cited by Pooley & Socolow and Bauer & Nadler; I did not read it, so nothing specific to Lubken’s argument beyond “straw man” (her chapter title) is claimed. The phrase “who said hypodermic first” in a search summary came from an unverifiable snippet and is not used.
- **Jansen’s chapter “Walter Lippmann, Straw Man of Communication Research”** (same volume as Lubken): known only from the LoC table of contents (machine-generated); not used.
- **Thibault, “Celestial Posters: Skywriting and the Folk Theory of Media Influence”** (ZeMKI Working Paper 37): read for the historiographic overview, not cited (undated working paper, I could not confirm the year).
- **Institute for Propaganda Analysis**: only what Pooley & Socolow say (a Progressive antipropaganda education group; Cantril was its founding board chair; it wanted a school version of the Welles study and Cantril blocked it). Founding year, “seven devices” and Sproule’s book *Propaganda and Democracy* were **not** verified and are not in the lesson.
