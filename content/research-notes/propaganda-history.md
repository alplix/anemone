## (0) Corrections and findings that change the obvious story

- **1622 vs 1627.** Bernays’s 1928 book (pp. 21–23) quotes a Scientific American piece that says the Roman body was “instituted at Rome in the year 1627”. The congregation dates from 1622 (Vatican; Catholic Encyclopedia: 6 January 1622 consistory, bull *Inscrutabili Divinae* of 22 June 1622); 1627 is the bull *Immortalis Dei* (1 August 1627) that founded the Collegium Urbanum, the seminary. The lesson uses the mix-up in the deep card `deep-word-1627` and does not claim to know how it arose.
- **The “Charteris story” is itself contested.** Lasswell (1927, pp. 206–207) reports as fact that Brigadier General Charteris swapped two photograph captions; his source is the New York Times of 20 October 1925. Neander & Marlin (2010) show that Charteris then denied or hedged, call his statements “less than fully candid”, found no trace of the letter he described in *The Field*, and argue that the Northcliffe press carried the story. The lesson therefore does not say who invented the corpse factory; it dates the publication (17 April 1917) and the withdrawal (2 December 1925) and gives the Charteris account as a contested tale (`deep-charteris`).
- **Neander & Marlin’s date.** The Times and Daily Mail of 17 April 1917; Ponsonby also prints a Times extract of 16 April 1917. The lesson uses 17 April for the joint publication (the source for the two texts side by side) and gives 10 April 1917 only as the Secretary for War’s 1925 date for the first appearance in a Berlin paper and two Belgian papers.
- **Definitions of white/grey/black differ.** The Turkish Language Association (TDK) defines “beyaz propaganda” as steering with *true* information whose source is visible; the English reference source (Osgood 2002; an academic-library online exhibit; Wikipedia) defines the three colours by the attribution of the source only. The lesson states the difference in the deep card. TDK has entries for beyaz and gri, none for kara or siyah propaganda.
- **Ellul’s “agitation” is not Lenin’s.** Ellul writes (in the text of the 1973 reissue) that his distinction “corresponds in part” to Lenin’s but that “the meaning of these terms is reversed”; the lesson keeps them apart (`deep-ellul-lenin`; the interpretation that the two authors cut the field on different axes is marked as ours).
- **Osgood’s thesis** that the negative charge of “propaganda” arose after World War I from a democracy’s (Britain’s) exposed atrocity stories, not from dictatorships, is presented as Osgood’s argument, supported by three dated documents (Creel 1920, Bernays 1928 quoting an older magazine, Etymonline) rather than as settled fact.

## (a) Could not verify, therefore left out (or explicitly hedged)

**Word and pre-modern material**
- **Oxford English Dictionary entry** (paywall/redirect): the lesson uses Etymonline for 1718, about 1790 and the WWI sense; I did not confirm the OED’s earliest date (a search result gave 1679/1623 for early evidence; not used).
- **“Gerundive” explanation** (propaganda = “things to be propagated”): appeared only in a search-result summary of the OED. Not used; the lesson says only “from *propagare*, to spread”.
- **French Revolution pamphleteering and Thomas Paine’s *Common Sense* (1776):** no source opened gave dates or circulation figures I could confirm twice, so both are left out. The Encyclopedia.com/Osgood entry mentions Paine as the Revolution’s most famous propagandist and Samuel Adams’s pseudonyms (twenty-five, single source); not used.
- **Franklin 1782:** documented by the Journal of the American Revolution (Harrington 2014) and hoaxes.org; the Founders Online edition returned no text; Osgood (2002) mentions a 1777 forged letter and “a forged issue of the Boston Independent” but dates the forgery differently from Harrington. The lesson uses Harrington’s 1782 account only and describes the content of the forged letter in general terms (“terrible cruelties”), without the stereotype of the original, on purpose.
- **Res Gestae at Ankara:** the Loeb introduction (1924) and Turkish Archaeological News agree on Latin and Greek versions, on the copy at Ancyra, and on Busbecq’s 1555 mission; the Loeb text writes “Buysbecche” and “Ferdinand II”, other sources say Ferdinand of Austria (Ferdinand I); the lesson says “Ferdinand’s ambassador”. Why the text was written in two languages is not stated in the sources I read; the lesson says so and marks the audience remark as our reading. The date of the inscription is given as after AD 14 (Turkish Archaeological News) and not more precisely. Britannica, the Ankara Culture pages and the World Monuments Fund listing could not be opened.

**World War I**
- **Wellington House:** the cabinet decision of 5 September 1914 rests on Sanders (1975; Cambridge extract) and on a blog quoting Sanders; the writers’ meeting of 2 September 1914, the “utmost secrecy” and “not public until 1935” come only from Wikipedia, a blog, HistoryNet and a search summary, so they are **left out**. The 2.5 million pamphlets in 17 languages by June 1915, the mailing list of 250,000 Americans and the 1,160 pamphlets are single-source or popular-source figures and are **left out**. Masterman’s name as head of the bureau is taken from HistoryNet and Wikipedia (both popular); Lasswell (1927) does not name him.
- **Department of Information (1917), Ministry of Information (1918), Crewe House (Northcliffe):** known only from Wikipedia here; not used.
- **German and Ottoman propaganda efforts:** left out entirely. I opened nothing solid on the German Zentralstelle für Auslandsdienst or on the Ottoman jihad proclamation of November 1914 and the propaganda around it. Osgood’s remark that German propaganda was probably less effective than British is not used either.
- **Genocide-related material (the Armenian case) and the British 1916 Blue Book on it:** deliberately **not** treated. I verified nothing about that document in this batch (its provenance, authorship or reception are from memory only and are not stated in the lesson). The truth of a claim about a genocide is a separate question from the propaganda use of a document, and a two-line treatment would risk being misused in either direction; it needs its own carefully sourced card that rests on the scholarship about the events.
- **Bryce Report:** the warrant of 15 December 1914, the seven signatories, more than 1,200 depositions, no authority to administer an oath, anonymised names, the rejection of hearsay and the conclusions come from the report’s own text (OCR scan). The publication date “12 May 1915” and the figures “41,000 copies to the US”, “translated into 30 languages” and the destruction of the original depositions by bombing appear only on Wikipedia and are **left out**. Wilson (1979), Lipkes (2007) and Bennett (2019) are named on Wikipedia; I opened none of them and cite none. Horne & Kramer (2001) was **not read**: the lesson relies on the Yale University Press description (about 6,500 civilian deaths; the franc-tireur delusion) and on the History Ireland article, which calls it revisionist and says it both debunks and redefines German atrocities and bolsters some fault lines in Bryce’s methodology. Individual massacres (Dinant, Louvain) are **left out**: sources gave different victim numbers (647/674 for Dinant).
- **Corpse factory:** the Hansard passages (24 November and 2 December 1925) and Lasswell’s and Ponsonby’s texts are first-hand. The story “China” route, the claim that the denial made Holocaust reports less credible (Osgood 2002; Neander & Marlin 2010) and the assertion that the story shaped the Treaty of Versailles (Neander & Marlin) are historians’ arguments; the lesson gives the first as an argument (`crit-whose`) and leaves out the second.
- **Creel Committee figures:** all Four Minute Men numbers (75,000 speakers; 5,200 communities; 7,555,190 speeches; 134,454,514 listeners) are Creel’s own; the lesson labels them so (`how-creel` and its mini question). The OCR shows one of them garbled (“755~190”); the second occurrence reads “7,555,190”. The 134 million audience figure is not used.
- **Bernays:** the claim in some sources that Goebbels owned or used Bernays’s *Crystallizing Public Opinion* was **not** verified and is left out. *Crystallizing Public Opinion* (1923), *The Engineering of Consent* (1947) and *Biography of an Idea* (1965) are named in biographical web pages but were not opened as books; the person page and the lesson name only *Propaganda* (1928). Bernays’s **death place** was not confirmed (New York in some pages), so `died` is left empty. The Lucky Strike “Torches of Freedom” episode (1929) is left out: it needs a proper source. Whether Bernays himself said he chose “public relations” to avoid the word propaganda: two web biographies say so; his own memoir was not opened, and the 1928 book carries both names, which is what the lesson says.
